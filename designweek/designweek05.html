Design Week 05 — Pump Pump Escape

A two-player minecart game controlled with bike pumps and a Makey Makey. This week pushed me to think beyond traditional inputs and design an experience built around communication, rhythm, and trust.

Design Challenge

The challenge for this week was to create a game that used a non-conventional controller. Instead of relying on a keyboard, mouse, or standard gamepad, the core of the experience had to come from a physical, unusual input device. Before pitching ideas, we explored games that already use unconventional controllers to understand how physical interaction can shape the feel of a game.

Initial Concepts

I started by exploring a few different directions that leaned into physicality and novelty:

  • Pop-Up Book Platformer: A platformer where each page of a pop-up book becomes a new level.
  • Saber-Cutting Game: A slicing game where the player uses a physical “saber” to cut through objects.
  • Barcode Fighting Game: A two-player fighter where scanning barcodes triggers different moves.

These ideas helped me think about how physical actions could be tied directly to in-game outcomes in a meaningful way.

Team Direction — The Minecart Idea

As a team, we gravitated toward a minecart-themed game that emphasized communication. We liked the idea of one player not being able to see the screen and having to rely entirely on the other player’s instructions to survive.

The core concept was simple but strong: one player would make decisions about the track and obstacles, while the other player would act on those instructions using a non-conventional controller.

My Proposal — Minecart of Madness

My proposal expanded the minecart idea into a more intense, physical experience. I wanted the game to feel chaotic, loud, and collaborative — the kind of experience where players are constantly talking, reacting, and laughing.

Controller Setup — Two Bike Pumps + Makey Makey

For the controller, we used a Makey Makey wired into two bike pumps. Both pumps acted as the main input method, and the Makey Makey translated the physical pumping into in-game actions.

The key mechanic was that both players had to alternate their pumps to go fast. Pumping at the same time slowed the cart down, while falling out of rhythm made the cart unstable. This turned the controller into a shared physical challenge rather than just a quirky input device.

Player Roles & Communication

The game relied heavily on communication, and that became the heart of the experience.

Driver — Player Facing the Screen

  • Sees the track, obstacles, and upcoming hazards.
  • Pumps in rhythm with the other player to keep the minecart moving.
  • Calls out instructions like “Up!”, “Down!”, or “Bomb!”.
  • Decides when to switch to the upper or lower track.
  • Warns the bomber when an obstacle is coming.

Bomber — Player Not Facing the Screen

  • Does not see the screen and relies entirely on the driver’s voice.
  • Pumps in rhythm to maintain speed.
  • Triggers bombs when the driver calls “Bomb!”.
  • Switches tracks when told “Up!” or “Down!”.

This setup created a natural sense of trust and tension. The driver had all the information, but the bomber had all the power to act. If communication broke down, the run usually did too.

Development — My Contributions

During development, I focused on the programming and technical setup of the controller and the game.

  • Hooked up the Makey Makey to Unity and tested each input.
  • Verified that the bike pumps reliably triggered the actions we needed.
  • Imported most of the art assets into Unity and organized them in the scene.
  • Set up the minecart for animation so it felt responsive and alive.

This week gave me a chance to lean into my technical side — wiring hardware, testing inputs, and making sure everything worked together inside Unity.

Final Game — Pump Pump Escape

The final game was called Pump Pump Escape, which fit the experience perfectly. Players had to:

  • Stay in rhythm with their pumping to keep the minecart moving.
  • Communicate clearly and quickly under pressure.
  • Time bombs correctly to destroy obstacles.
  • Switch tracks at the right moment to avoid danger.
  • Escape the caves before the time runs out.

The result was a loud, energetic, and surprisingly strategic game that felt very different from anything you could play with a normal controller.

Takeaway

This project taught me how much a controller can define the entire feel of a game. Designing around bike pumps and a Makey Makey forced me to think about rhythm, communication, and physical effort as core mechanics. It also reinforced how much I enjoy working at the intersection of design, programming, and unconventional interaction.
