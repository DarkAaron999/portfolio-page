Design Week 05 — Non-Conventional Controller Minecart Game

A two-player minecart game controlled with bike pumps and a Makey Makey. This week pushed me to think beyond traditional inputs and design an experience built around communication, rhythm, and trust.

Design Challenge: The challenge for this week was to create a game that used a non-conventional controller. Instead of relying on a keyboard, mouse, or standard gamepad, the core of the experience had to come from a physical, unusual input device. Before pitching ideas, we looked at existing games that use unconventional controllers to understand how physical interaction can shape the feel of a game.

Initial Concepts

I started by exploring a few different directions that leaned into physicality and novelty:

  • Pop-Up Book Platformer: A platformer where each page of a pop-up book is a different level. Turning the page reveals a new environment for the player to explore.
  • Saber-Cutting Game: A slicing game where the player uses a “saber” to cut through boxes, food, wood, and other objects, focusing on angle and timing.
  • Barcode Fighting Game: A two-player fighting game where players scan barcodes to execute moves, with each barcode triggering a different attack or ability.

These ideas helped me think about how physical actions could be tied directly to in-game outcomes in a meaningful way.

Team Direction — The Minecart Idea

As a team, we gravitated toward a minecart-themed game that emphasized communication. We liked the idea of one player not being able to see the screen and having to rely on the other player’s instructions to survive.

The core concept was simple but strong: one player would be responsible for making decisions about the track and when to blow up obstacles, while the other player would act on those instructions using a non-conventional controller.

My Proposal — Minecart of Madness

My proposal took the minecart idea and pushed it into a more intense, physical experience called Minecart of Madness. It was designed as a two-player game where both players shared responsibility for keeping the minecart moving and staying alive.

I wanted the game to feel chaotic, loud, and collaborative — the kind of experience where players are constantly talking, reacting, and laughing.

Controller Setup — Two Bike Pumps + Makey Makey

For the controller, we used a Makey Makey wired into two bike pumps. Both pumps acted as the main input method, and the Makey Makey translated the physical pumping into in-game actions.

The key mechanic was that both players had to alternate their pumps to go fast. If they pumped at the same time or fell out of rhythm, the minecart would slow down or feel unstable. This turned the controller into a shared physical challenge rather than just a quirky input device.

Player Roles & Communication

The game relied heavily on communication, and that became the heart of the experience.

Driver — Player Facing the Screen

  • Faces the screen and can see the track, obstacles, and upcoming hazards.
  • Pumps in rhythm with the other player to keep the minecart moving.
  • Calls out instructions like “Up!”, “Down!”, or “Bomb!” to the other player.
  • Decides when to switch to the upper or lower track.
  • Warns the other player when an obstacle is coming.

Bomber — Player Not Facing the Screen

  • Does not see the screen at all and relies entirely on the driver’s voice.
  • Also pumps to maintain speed, staying in rhythm with the driver.
  • When the driver calls “Bomb!”, they trigger the bomb input to destroy an obstacle.
  • When the driver calls “Up!” or “Down!”, they switch the track accordingly.

This setup created a natural sense of trust and tension. The driver had all the information, but the bomber had all the power to act. If communication broke down, the run usually did too.

Development — My Contributions

During development, I focused on the programming and technical setup of the controller and the game.

  • Worked on hooking up the Makey Makey to Unity and testing what each input mapped to.
  • Verified that the bike pumps could reliably trigger the actions we needed in-game.
  • Imported most of the art assets into Unity and organized them in the scene.
  • Set up the minecart for animation so it felt responsive and alive during gameplay.

This week gave me a chance to lean into my technical side — wiring up hardware, testing inputs, and making sure everything worked together inside Unity.

Final Game — Bomb & Pump

The final game was called Bomb & Pump, which fit the experience perfectly. Players had to:

  • Stay in rhythm with their pumping to keep the minecart moving.
  • Communicate clearly and quickly under pressure.
  • Time bombs correctly to destroy obstacles.
  • Switch tracks at the right moment to avoid danger.

The result was a loud, energetic, and surprisingly strategic game that felt very different from anything you could play with a normal controller.

Takeaway

This project taught me how much a controller can define the entire feel of a game. Designing around bike pumps and a Makey Makey forced me to think about rhythm, communication, and physical effort as core mechanics. It also reinforced how much I enjoy working at the intersection of design, programming, and unconventional interaction.
